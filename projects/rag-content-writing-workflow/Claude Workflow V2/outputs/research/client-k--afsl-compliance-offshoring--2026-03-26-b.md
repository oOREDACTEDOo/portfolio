## 7. Beat Strategy Detail

**Point 1 — Named enforcement: make the risk concrete**
Client B is the most powerful example: a Federal Court ordered $2.5M under the exact same general AFS licence obligations (s912A) that govern offshore outsourcing governance. This is not a hypothetical. The court said this "sets a clear licence-to-operate expectation." ASIC Deputy Chair Sarah Court confirmed this momentum continues. Most benchmark articles mention enforcement action abstractly; naming Client B with the dollar figure is a significant differentiator.

**Point 2 — The model distinction (BPO black-box vs. staff augmentation)**
ASIC's review specifically examined intermediaries connecting licensees to Philippines, India, and Sri Lanka. The governance failures ASIC found — no visibility into OSP access, no audit capability, no monitoring of who is doing what with client data — are characteristics of the BPO/intermediary model, not the dedicated staff augmentation model. This structural distinction is the article's core argument and no benchmark article makes it.

**Point 3 — Board-level governance**
Clayton Utz is explicit: "board-level engagement and ownership is essential." This elevates the article beyond a compliance officer's checklist into a C-suite conversation. AFSL holders cannot delegate this to a compliance manager and consider it handled.

**Point 4 — "What good looks like" (ASIC's 9 areas)**
Clayton Utz identifies that ASIC has published 9 areas of better risk management for OSPs. Using this as a section structure ("What ASIC says good looks like") gives the article an authoritative, regulatory-backed framework that positions Client K's model against ASIC's own benchmarks — not just Client K's claims.

**Point 5 — The Client K executive voice on set-and-forget**
Same as previous run: "If you think it's outsourcing and set and forget, it's not going to work." Plus the governance mindset quote and compound advantage quote.
